Modernizing a legacy system takes more than moving it to AWS. Teams also need to understand the application, choose an appropriate change path, and operate the result securely and reliably. Training helps when it prepares people for those specific responsibilities. It does not guarantee lower costs, faster migrations, or a particular return on investment; those outcomes depend on the workload, implementation choices, and business context, and must be measured.

Start with the modernization work in front of you, then identify which skills the people doing that work need to demonstrate. AWS guidance treats workforce planning as part of cloud adoption and recommends assessing role and skill gaps before building a targeted learning strategy. See the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework People perspective.

Let modernization outcomes set the learning agenda

First agree on what must improve or remain true for the system: supported business workflows, availability and recovery needs, security and compliance controls, performance, operating effort, and cost constraints. Record how the system works today, including its users, data, dependencies, interfaces, scheduled jobs, and operational owners. AWS recommends assessing applications’ business, functional, technical, and financial significance as part of modernization readiness; its modernization readiness guide describes that process.

Use that evidence to choose a path for each application. AWS describes seven common migration strategies: retire, retain, rehost, relocate, repurchase, replatform, and refactor or re-architect. They involve different work, so they call for different learning. A rehost may emphasize inventory, dependencies, landing-zone controls, and cutover rehearsals. A refactor may also require application design, automated testing, deployment, and operations skills. The AWS migration strategies guide explains the options and their tradeoffs.

Do not train everyone on every service. Prioritize the skills needed for the selected path and the system’s known gaps. For example, when a team is assessing an unfamiliar codebase, this site’s legacy code analysis guide can help frame the evidence to gather before choosing a migration approach.

Assess skills by role and task

List the concrete tasks in the modernization plan and the people accountable for each one. Then compare current experience with what each task requires. Include transferable skills: operating systems, networking, databases, software delivery, incident response, and business-process knowledge remain useful. Assess how they apply in the proposed AWS environment rather than treating past experience as either sufficient or irrelevant.

Use short interviews, work samples, and a small practical exercise to establish a baseline. Record who can perform each task independently, who needs guidance, and where no owner exists. AWS Skill Builder offers training organized by role and domain; use its current AWS training catalog to find material that matches the gaps. Courses and credentials can structure learning, but completing them alone does not show that someone can operate your particular system.

Build role-based learning paths

Give the project team a shared foundation, then add focused learning for each role. A useful sequence is:

  1. Common cloud foundations: AWS account and Region concepts, the shared responsibility model, identity and access basics, networking, compute, storage, databases, and the organization’s security and cost controls.
  2. Role-specific practice: developers work on deployment and testing; operators work on monitoring and recovery; data teams work on transfer and validation; security teams review access and evidence; owners refine workflow and acceptance criteria.
  3. Workload-specific learning: focus on the runtime, AWS services, migration pattern, integrations, and operational procedures selected for the application.
  4. Practice with the delivery team: pair less-experienced staff with maintainers or cloud practitioners during design reviews, rehearsals, and supervised production support.

Choose depth according to responsibility. A developer who edits infrastructure needs to understand the organization’s review and access process; they may not need to design the network. A system owner should understand recovery objectives and operational tradeoffs without needing to configure every service. Certifications can be useful when a role calls for a broad, externally validated body of knowledge, but make them optional evidence alongside practical work.

Practice modernization in a controlled environment

Make exercises resemble real work while keeping their impact bounded. Use an organization-approved non-production account or isolated environment with synthetic or approved test data, least-privilege access, cost monitoring, an owner, and a cleanup date. Keep production credentials and sensitive production data out of training exercises. Confirm which Regions and services the environment allows before learners create resources.

Pick one representative slice of the application and define a reviewable exercise. Depending on the chosen migration path, the team might:

  • map its dependencies and deployment steps;
  • provision a small, repeatable environment from reviewed infrastructure as code;
  • deploy the application and check its identity, network, and data access;
  • run a known workflow while using logs, metrics, or traces to investigate a failure;
  • rehearse a test-data migration, backup restore, cutover, or rollback; and
  • review the resulting security evidence, resource use, and cleanup.

Keep the exercise tied to a question the project needs answered. A small pilot on a representative application slice can expose missing permissions, undocumented dependencies, or a weak recovery procedure before the team applies the approach more broadly. Use what you learn to decide whether to expand the pilot, adjust the approach, or gather more evidence. For guidance on shared ownership and review of infrastructure changes, see this site’s AWS infrastructure as code pitfalls guide.

Make learning part of delivery

Schedule practice alongside discovery, design, and migration milestones. Reserve time for pairing, code and architecture reviews, migration rehearsals, and post-exercise review. Ask participants to update the runbook, test suite, diagrams, and ownership records as they learn. This leaves reusable evidence for the next person instead of concentrating knowledge in one specialist.

Review obstacles with the team. If people cannot practice because there is no safe environment, resolve that access and ownership issue. If a course does not match the application, replace it with a focused exercise. If a critical task has no owner, assign an accountable role and a learning plan before scheduling the related migration work.

Measure progress through work evidence

Set evidence criteria before training begins. They should follow the application’s requirements, not a generic number of courses or badges. Examples include whether the responsible staff can:

  • explain a critical workflow and its documented dependencies;
  • deploy a reviewed change using the approved process and permissions;
  • diagnose a seeded failure using the available operational signals;
  • complete and validate a test migration or restore against the application’s recovery objectives; and
  • show that required access, logging, encryption, and cleanup controls are in place.

Repeat the relevant exercise after feedback and record the remaining gaps, owner, and next check. Separately track workload outcomes such as successful workflow tests, recovery results, security findings, migration defects, or measured operating cost. A skills assessment can show whether the team is ready for defined work; it cannot by itself prove that modernization has improved cost, reliability, or business results.

Conclusion

Effective upskilling begins with the application and the people responsible for changing and operating it. Set modernization outcomes, assess skills by role, teach only what the selected work requires, and practice on a controlled slice of the system. Use demonstrated delivery and operational evidence to decide what the team can take on next, and keep business results separate from course completion.
