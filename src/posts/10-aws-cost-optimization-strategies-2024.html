AWS cost optimization is a recurring engineering and finance practice: understand what drives spend, assign costs to the work that creates them, and change capacity or purchasing choices only after checking performance and service requirements. AWS recommendations and cost estimates help identify options; measure the result in your own workload before treating projected savings as realized savings.

1. Get a clear view of AWS spending

Start with the Billing and Cost Management home page and AWS Cost Explorer to compare spend over time, group it by service, account, Region, usage type, or other supported dimensions, and investigate material changes. Use Cost Optimization Hub to bring supported rightsizing, idle-resource, and commitment recommendations together. Recommendations are estimates based on available usage and pricing data; check their assumptions and validate the effect after making a change.

For detailed line items, joins, or custom analysis, export Cost and Usage Report 2.0 (CUR 2.0) through AWS Data Exports. It is AWS's recommended way to receive detailed cost and usage data. Use the Bills page and invoice for billing records; analytical views can group or display costs differently.

2. Attribute costs and assign owners

Decide which business dimensions matter—such as application, team, environment, or cost center—and apply a consistent scheme across accounts and supported resources. Activate user-defined or AWS-generated cost allocation tags in Billing and Cost Management before expecting them in Cost Explorer or cost reports. Tags can take time to appear, and some shared or service-level charges do not map cleanly to an individual resource.

Use AWS Cost Categories to group or split charges according to your organization's structure when resource tags alone are not enough. Track unallocated spend as its own item so that better coverage can be treated as work to do, rather than assuming every charge is already assigned.

Coordinate cost work across teams

Finance or a FinOps function can coordinate forecasts, shared-cost allocation, and commitment analysis with engineering and product teams. A separate specialist team may fit some organizations, but each workload still needs an owner who understands its cost drivers and can act on agreed changes.

Assign an owner to each workload

Name an engineering or product owner for each workload and connect that ownership to the account, tags, or cost category used in reports. Make the owner responsible for explaining material changes and checking that an optimization preserves the workload's performance and recovery needs.

3. Audit for idle and unused resources

Review your inventory for resources that remain provisioned without serving a workload: stopped or forgotten development environments, unattached volumes, obsolete snapshots, idle databases, unused load balancers, and other retained capacity. Cost Optimization Hub and AWS Compute Optimizer can surface supported idle-resource recommendations, but confirm ownership, dependencies, recovery needs, and retention requirements before stopping or deleting anything.

Why review idle resources?

An idle resource may still incur charges for its compute, storage, addresses, requests, or attached services. Stopping compute does not necessarily remove charges for attached storage or other related resources. Inventory the full resource chain, identify its owner, and choose whether to stop, resize, schedule, or delete it.

How to run an audit

  1. Group resources by account, tags, service, and environment to find items without an owner or current workload.
  2. Compare utilization and cost data over a period that includes expected peaks and normal operating cycles.
  3. Confirm data retention, backup, recovery, and application dependencies with the owner.
  4. Make the smallest safe change, then check service health and the next cost report.

4. Right-size against measured workload needs

Use representative utilization and performance data to choose instance families, sizes, storage performance, and service capacity. Look beyond average CPU: memory, network, storage I/O, queue depth, concurrency, and application latency can constrain a workload even when CPU appears low. Include seasonal peaks and recovery capacity in the analysis.

Why right-size?

Oversized resources can waste spend, while undersized resources can increase latency, errors, retries, or missed service objectives. Treat a recommendation as a candidate change: compare its estimated savings and performance risk, test it where practical, and monitor the production result.

How to right-size

Use Compute Optimizer or Cost Optimization Hub to find supported opportunities, then validate them against workload metrics and application behavior. Auto Scaling can add or remove instances as demand changes; it does not automatically resize each instance to a different type. Recheck scaling minimums, maximums, and warm capacity so that savings do not come at the expense of required availability or response time.

5. Match purchase options to stable and variable usage

Use On-Demand pricing while a workload is new, changing quickly, or difficult to forecast. Consider a commitment only after reviewing normalized usage, expected changes, current discounts, and existing commitments. AWS provides recommendations and purchase analysis in Billing and Cost Management; compare utilization and coverage as well as projected savings.

Savings Plans exchange a commitment for discounted eligible usage. Current plan types differ in coverage:

Database eligibility depends on service, instance generation, and deployment type. Check the current AWS Savings Plans FAQ and service coverage update for exact usage filters and recent additions. The Savings Plans commitment is an hourly amount at the plan rate, not an On-Demand usage target. Unused commitment still costs money, usage beyond it is billed at the applicable On-Demand rate, and the term and commitment cannot be changed after purchase.

For EC2, Reserved Instances and Savings Plans have different benefits. EC2 Reserved Instances are one- or three-year purchases: regional RIs discount qualifying usage but do not reserve capacity, while zonal RIs provide a discount and reserve capacity for matched usage in a specified Availability Zone. Savings Plans provide discounts on eligible usage but do not reserve EC2 capacity. If capacity assurance is a separate requirement, evaluate EC2 Capacity Reservations and their billing independently.

Use EC2 Spot Instances only for interruptible work that can checkpoint, retry, or otherwise tolerate capacity loss. EC2 generally sends an interruption notice two minutes before stopping or terminating an instance, but notices are best effort; hibernation begins immediately without that two-minute lead time. Keep interruption-sensitive workloads on suitable capacity and test recovery behavior.

6. Use budgets as alerts and planning signals

Track actual and forecast spend

Create AWS Budgets for overall spend and useful slices such as an account, service, tag, or cost category. Configure actual-spend and forecasted-spend thresholds so owners can investigate early. A budget is not a universal spending cap: AWS Budgets can run configured actions at a threshold, but those actions require permissions and apply only to supported resources or policies.

Set thresholds that prompt useful action

Choose thresholds that leave time to investigate the cause and make a safe decision. Route each alert to someone who can inspect the workload, deployment, and billing data. Keep a budget aligned with an agreed scope and time period so the alert can lead to a concrete response.

Review forecasts and assumptions

Use forecasts to spot possible budget pressure, not as a promise of the final bill. Forecasts are based on historical cost patterns and may miss launches, traffic changes, one-time migrations, or changes to discounts. Revisit the baseline when the workload or business plan changes.

7. Detect unusual spend and investigate it

Set up cost monitors

AWS Cost Anomaly Detection analyzes billing data for unusual spend patterns. Because it uses processed billing data, an anomaly can take up to 24 hours to detect after usage. Use service metrics and operational alerts when a workload needs faster detection.

Route anomaly alerts to an owner

Configure monitor thresholds and alert subscriptions for the people who can investigate spend changes. An anomaly alert helps identify a change; it does not stop usage.

Trace the cost driver

When an anomaly appears, compare its service, linked account, Region, and usage type with recent deployments and traffic. Use Cost Explorer for a fast breakdown and CUR 2.0 when you need line-item detail. Record the cause, owner, and follow-up change so a one-time spike can improve monitoring or capacity planning.

8. Review data-transfer and network costs

Break network charges down by source, destination, Region, and usage type before redesigning traffic paths. Cross-Region transfer may be necessary for resilience, data residency, or latency goals, so assess the complete architecture and current AWS pricing rather than moving traffic solely to chase a lower transfer rate.

Reduce traffic that does not serve the workload

Check whether large transfers can be compressed, deduplicated, or batched without changing the application's correctness or retention needs. Keep data near the services that use it only when that choice also meets resilience, latency, and residency requirements.

Compare CloudFront caching and pricing

CloudFront can reduce repeated origin requests for cacheable content. Compare its current pay-as-you-go and flat-rate plans with the rest of the design: pay-as-you-go pricing includes edge data transfer and request charges, while flat-rate plans bundle services and allowances. Caching alone does not guarantee a lower total bill.

Compare network paths before changing them

A NAT Gateway has hourly and processed-data charges. If much of its traffic is to AWS services that support VPC endpoints, compare endpoint costs and routing requirements with the current NAT path. Do not replace a private network path with public IP addresses solely to avoid a NAT charge; include security, availability, and other transfer costs in the decision. See AWS guidance on NAT Gateway pricing and CloudFront pricing.

9. Match storage and balancing capacity to the workload

Review load-balancing capacity

Load balancers add cost, but also provide routing, health checks, and availability. Review traffic, capacity, idle periods, and service requirements before changing them. Consolidate or remove a load balancer only when the resulting architecture still meets isolation, security, and resilience needs.

Choose storage for access and retention needs

Select storage classes and volume performance based on access frequency, retrieval time, availability, durability, and recovery objectives—not the storage rate alone. Lifecycle rules can move or expire objects based on defined age, prefix, or tag criteria. Include transition requests, retrieval charges, and class-specific minimum storage durations in the comparison. Under current S3 Lifecycle defaults, objects smaller than 128 KB do not transition to another storage class; existing configurations created before the default changed may behave differently. Each transition has a request cost, so validate the actual rule against your object sizes and access pattern.

For more detail on S3 performance choices that can affect cost, see 10 Amazon S3 Performance Optimization Tips. The right choice depends on whether the workload is constrained by storage access, request volume, or data movement.

Check the current S3 storage-class comparison and guidance on Lifecycle transition costs and constraints before changing retention or archive rules.

10. Review software licensing separately

Separate compute sizing from license charges

Changing an instance size or purchase plan does not automatically reduce a software vendor's license charges. AWS Compute Optimizer supports recommendations for some commercial software licenses; treat them as candidates to investigate, not a substitute for reviewing contract terms.

Check eligibility before using BYOL

Compare license-included and eligible Bring Your Own License (BYOL) options only after checking the software vendor's terms, AWS licensing rules, workload topology, and support requirements.

Model licensing options with AWS OLA

The AWS Optimization and Licensing Assessment (OLA) can help model resource use, dependencies, and licensing scenarios. Treat an assessment as input to a licensing decision, not as confirmation that a license option is eligible.

Conclusion

Start with reliable cost data and clear ownership, then work through idle resources, sizing, commitments, storage, and network paths. For each change, write down the expected effect, protect performance and recovery requirements, and check the actual result. Set a review cadence that fits the rate of change in your environment, revisit commitments before renewal, and compare realized costs with the baseline. Repeat the review when workload behavior, service choices, or AWS pricing models change.
