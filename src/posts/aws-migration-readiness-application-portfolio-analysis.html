Application portfolio analysis helps answer three migration questions: what is in scope, what should happen to each workload, and what should move together. It combines business context with technical evidence from inventory, usage, dependencies, and application owners. It is an iterative planning activity, not a one-time scan or an automatic verdict that an application is ready for AWS.

Portfolio analysis is one part of migration readiness. The AWS Migration Readiness Assessment (MRA) looks at the organization through the six AWS Cloud Adoption Framework perspectives: business, people, governance, platform, security, and operations. Portfolio assessment asks workload-level questions. A migration can need both: a useful workload plan and the organizational capabilities to carry it out.

A practical assessment in brief

  1. Define business outcomes, scope, owners, and constraints.
  2. Build an application and infrastructure inventory from existing records, tools, and people.
  3. Record how complete, recent, and trustworthy each important data point is.
  4. Validate technical and business dependencies, then assess near-term candidates in more detail.
  5. Choose a migration strategy for each application, model costs and benefits, and group related work into feasible waves.
  6. Update the portfolio plan as discovery improves and migration waves reveal new information.

A portfolio view supports prioritization; it does not replace a detailed application assessment, target design, security review, or cutover plan. AWS describes portfolio assessment as a continuous process that grows from initial discovery to higher-fidelity planning over time in its application portfolio assessment guide.

Separate portfolio analysis from readiness reviews

“Readiness” can refer to different things. Keeping these questions separate prevents a server inventory or a broad organizational review from being mistaken for proof that an individual application can move safely.

  • Organizational readiness: Can teams govern, secure, operate, fund, and support the cloud program? An MRA uses AWS CAF to identify organizational strengths, gaps, and actions. It does not decide the migration strategy or prove that each application is technically ready.
  • Portfolio assessment: Which applications and infrastructure are in scope, who owns them, how important are they, what are the likely migration strategies, and how could the work be sequenced?
  • Application assessment: For a workload selected for near-term migration, what are its architecture, versions, interfaces, data needs, licensing, security and compliance requirements, performance and recovery targets, and test and cutover approach?

Use the portfolio view to decide which workloads deserve a detailed assessment first. Revisit both workload and organizational readiness as new risks, dependencies, or resource constraints emerge.

Build an evidence-based portfolio

Create an inventory with clear ownership

Give every application a stable identifier and accountable business and technical owners. Capture its purpose, users, environment, lifecycle, criticality, release calendar, and known constraints. Link applications to the servers, databases, storage, networks, third-party services, and teams that support them.

Useful sources include a configuration management database (CMDB), asset and monitoring exports, network records, architecture diagrams, contracts and license records, support histories, and interviews with application owners. Record where each fact came from, when it was checked, and whether it is measured, documented, or an estimate. If sources disagree, keep the disagreement visible until an owner resolves it.

Collect technical and business evidence

For each application, gather the information needed to choose and plan a strategy. A concise first pass should cover:

  • Business context: business function, users, accountable owner, criticality, expected outcome, service hours, and dates to avoid.
  • Technology and lifecycle: software and operating-system versions, database engines, hardware or platform constraints, vendor support dates, license terms, and planned replacements.
  • Usage and capacity: CPU, memory, storage, throughput, growth, and relevant peaks or batch periods. Note the observation window; a short sample can miss seasonal or infrequent jobs.
  • Dependencies: application calls, server-to-server traffic, shared data, identity, certificates, DNS, scheduled jobs, external APIs, and operational handoffs.
  • Controls and service needs: data classification and location, security requirements, recovery time and recovery point objectives, performance needs, backup and restore, monitoring, and support ownership.

These fields are a starting set, not a universal checklist. Scale the depth to the decision: an early directional business case can use estimates with stated assumptions, while a committed migration wave needs higher-confidence data and workload-specific validation. AWS provides broader portfolio data requirements for teams building a detailed inventory and business case.

Treat discovery output as evidence, not completeness

Discovery tools can reduce manual inventory work and reveal observed server relationships. Their results still depend on which environments were included, what permissions and collectors were configured, and when measurements were taken. They do not automatically identify every application, owner, business dependency, contract, or readiness risk.

For example, AWS Transform's network collection can help map observed server-to-server connections. Its documentation says the default collection includes TCP IPv4 connections between servers in the discovered or imported inventory; connections to outside or undiscovered endpoints are omitted by default. Configure coverage deliberately, then compare tool results with application-owner knowledge, architecture records, and other available network evidence. A missing connection in a map does not prove that two systems are independent. See AWS's description of AWS Transform data collection.

Separate measured facts from assumptions and record gaps that could change the strategy or wave. This makes the next investigation useful: collect more data where uncertainty could affect cost, compatibility, security, downtime, or workload grouping.

Use people and records to fill the gaps

Start with existing records

Manual discovery is useful when the source estate is small, automated access is restricted, or business context is not present in technical tools. Reuse existing records first, then ask owners targeted questions to confirm purpose, usage patterns, interfaces, known failure modes, business dates, and who can approve a migration or retirement.

Validate uncertain relationships

Compare owner interviews and diagrams with observed traffic, logs, configuration, and deployment records. Ask about low-frequency activity such as month-end processing, annual reporting, disaster recovery tests, vendor support, and manual handoffs. Mark a relationship as confirmed, observed, inferred, or unresolved. This is more useful than presenting every connection as equally certain.

Combine automated discovery with owner review

A hybrid approach usually makes the best use of available evidence: import or collect infrastructure and utilization data programmatically, then ask owners and engineers to explain business meaning and verify missing or surprising details. Reconcile identifiers between tools so that a server, database, and business application can be followed through the same inventory.

Keep a record of data coverage, collection dates, assumptions, and open questions. Refresh the evidence when infrastructure changes or when a migration wave approaches. The portfolio should become more precise as decisions become more specific.

Choose current AWS tools for the question at hand

Migration Evaluator: a directional business case

AWS Migration Evaluator helps build a business case for running on-premises workloads on AWS. It can use inventory and utilization collected by its agentless collector or imported from other tools, then model projected costs and scenarios. Its output is only as useful as the scope, measurement period, licensing, and assumptions behind it. Treat a projection as an estimate to review, not as a guaranteed saving or a complete application readiness assessment. AWS describes Migration Evaluator's scope and inputs in its feature guide.

Application Discovery Service, Migration Hub, and AWS Transform

AWS Application Discovery Service and AWS Migration Hub stopped accepting new customers on November 7, 2025; existing customers can continue using the services for current projects. AWS recommends AWS Transform for new discovery and migration-planning projects. Check the current AWS service guidance for eligibility and supported workloads before selecting a tool. See the Application Discovery Service availability notice and the Migration Hub availability notice.

AWS Transform's discovery tool supports server inventory from sources including VMware, Hyper-V, and imported server data. Its migration planning can group servers into applications and waves, but the result still needs review against your business rules, technical evidence, and migration capacity. In particular, confirm that discovery includes the platforms and endpoints that matter to your application.

Compare tools by coverage and limits

There is no general accuracy or effort ranking that applies to every estate. Compare a tool against the question you need to answer: which platforms it can discover, which measurements it collects, the permissions and setup it needs, its data window, and the output format available for analysis. Manual work can supply context the tooling cannot see; tools can expose inconsistencies that interviews or static records miss.

Choose a migration strategy for each application

The 7 Rs describe migration strategies, not an automatic score of readiness. Choose based on business outcomes, technical constraints, cost, risk, time, and available skills. A single application may need different treatment for its application code, database, and data.

For AWS's fuller definitions and examples, see its guide to the 7 Rs. Record the reason, assumptions, owner, confidence, and conditions for revisiting each decision.

Build a defensible business case

Compare like with like over the same time period. The current-state baseline may include hardware and facilities, software and support licenses, staffing, network and recovery capacity, and the cost to operate current systems. The AWS scenarios may include compute, storage, databases, networking and data transfer, monitoring, security, support, licensing, and the labor to migrate and operate the target.

Include one-time migration work, training, landing-zone and tooling costs, parallel operation during transition, decommissioning, and risks that have a credible cost impact. State the Region, utilization period, purchase options, right-sizing assumptions, and what is excluded. Model more than one plausible scenario when pricing, demand, schedule, or refactoring effort is uncertain. Separate financial savings from benefits such as resilience, delivery speed, or reduced data-center exposure, and show the evidence behind each.

Replace early benchmarks with measured inventory and validated assumptions as the plan matures. Migration Evaluator can inform a directional server-cost comparison; it does not include every application, migration, organizational, or operational cost without the relevant data and analysis.

Turn assessment into migration waves

A wave groups applications and infrastructure that must move together or can be tested and operated together. Use dependencies, business criticality, technical complexity, data volume, release calendars, cutover and rollback constraints, team capacity, and platform, security, and operations readiness to form groups and set order. Keep a clear owner for every application and wave.

  1. Start from the business outcomes and identify migration candidates.
  2. Group tightly coupled systems and flag external or unresolved dependencies.
  3. Check that each proposed wave fits the migration team's capacity, available change windows, and the readiness of the target environment and support teams.
  4. Assess near-term candidates in detail, including target design, data movement, testing, cutover, rollback, and operational handover.
  5. Run a suitable pilot, record lessons, and update estimates, decisions, and later waves.

Prioritization is portfolio-specific. A low-criticality workload might be a useful early pilot, but only if its dependencies and operational needs are understood. Avoid assuming that low traffic or simple infrastructure automatically means low business risk. AWS's wave-planning guidance likewise combines dependency data, business drivers, cost and risk, skills, and platform readiness rather than applying a universal size or schedule.

When a wave is approved, plan data movement and cutover as separate work. The AWS content migration best practices guide covers transfer choices, destination verification, and rollback considerations for files, objects, and databases.

Frequently asked questions

What does application portfolio analysis produce?

It produces a maintained view of applications and supporting infrastructure, key ownership and business attributes, known dependencies and data gaps, migration strategy decisions, a costed business case, and a migration wave plan. The required detail grows as workloads move closer to migration.

Does a discovery tool prove an application is ready to migrate?

No. A tool can provide valuable inventory, utilization, and observed connection data, but it may not cover every system, business dependency, operating condition, or compliance requirement. Confirm coverage and validate important findings with owners and workload-specific assessment.

What are the 7 Rs of migration?

Retire, retain, rehost, relocate, repurchase, replatform, and refactor or re-architect. Relocate generally moves infrastructure or supported resources without redesigning the application. Repurchase replaces the application with another product or version, often SaaS.

Is Migration Evaluator a migration readiness assessment?

Migration Evaluator supports a directional business case by analyzing current workload information and projected AWS costs. Organizational readiness is addressed through an MRA aligned to AWS CAF; application readiness needs a workload-specific technical and business assessment.
